# Thesis-Driven Essay Structure

## Overview

A thesis-driven essay makes a single, arguable claim and defends it through
evidence, counter-positions, and synthesis. The goal is not to survey a topic
but to persuade a specific audience of a specific position.

Default length: 3,500 words (adjustable from 3,000 to 8,000).
Section weights are approximate percentages of total word count.

---

## Structure

### 1. Hook (3-5%)

Open with a scene, a counterintuitive fact, a question, or a story that makes
the reader unable to stop. The hook should create a gap — something the reader
wants to close by reading further.

**Techniques**:
- A specific, concrete scene (not a platitude or generalization)
- A surprising statistic ("X% of Y do Z, but almost no one talks about why")
- A short story that ends with an unresolved question
- A direct provocation ("The thing everyone believes about X is wrong")

**Avoid**: "In today's fast-paced world..." or any opening that could appear
in any essay about any topic.

### 2. Context and Stakes (5-8%)

Establish why this matters to this specific audience right now. Define the
scope without burying the reader in background. Answer: why should I keep
reading?

### 3. Thesis Statement (1-2%)

One to three sentences stating the central claim. The thesis must be:
- Arguable (not obviously true to everyone)
- Specific (not "X is complex" but "X fails because of Y")
- Falsifiable (a reader could, in principle, disagree with evidence)

Place the thesis at the end of the context section, before the first pillar.

### 4. Pillar Arguments (60-70%)

Three to five main arguments that support the thesis. Each pillar:

1. **Topic sentence**: a mini-thesis for this section (one sentence).
2. **Evidence**: data, examples, quotes, or research that support the claim.
   Be specific — vague references erode credibility.
3. **Explanation**: connect the evidence to the pillar claim explicitly.
4. **Transition**: a bridge sentence that connects this pillar to the next.

**Pillar order**: lead with your second-strongest argument, put your weakest
in the middle, and close with your strongest. This structure creates momentum
and ends on the most persuasive note.

### 5. Counter-Position (10-15%)

Steel-man the strongest objection to your thesis. Acknowledge its validity,
then explain why your thesis holds despite it. Essays that ignore objections
are weaker than essays that engage them.

Structure:
- "The strongest argument against this view is..."
- "This is true, and yet..." or "This objection misses..."

### 6. Synthesis (8-12%)

Bring the pillars together into a conclusion that is more than a summary.
The synthesis should feel like an arrival — the reader now understands
something they could not have without reading the essay.

### 7. Call-to-Action (2-3%)

Tell the reader what to do, think, or investigate next. This is not a sales
pitch — it is the practical implication of the thesis.

---

## Citation Discipline

- Cite specific sources for all data and research claims.
- For proprietary research or estimates, state the methodology.
- Use hyperlinks in digital essays; numbered endnotes in PDF/print.
- Distinguish between "evidence" (data, research) and "illustration" (anecdotes,
  examples). Both are valid; conflating them weakens arguments.

---

## Title Guidelines

A strong essay title:
- States or implies the thesis (not just the topic)
- Creates curiosity without being clickbait
- Is specific enough that the reader knows what to expect

**Formulas that work**:
- "[Counterintuitive claim]: [how the essay resolves it]"
- "Why [assumed thing] is [actually something else]"
- "The [specific problem] with [common practice]"
- "[Verb]-ing [topic]: [what the reader will learn or gain]"

Avoid:
- Question titles that the essay does not definitively answer
- "X Things About Y" listicle titles (signals low-rigor)
- Titles with adjectives that require the essay to earn ("The Definitive Guide")
